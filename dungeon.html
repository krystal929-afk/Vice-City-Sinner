Who the Fuck Invented the Dungeon?

How leather communities, private clubs, DIY furniture, safer-sex activism, and the internet built the modern play space.

By Satanica Lux · The Vice City Sinner

Walk into a lot of modern BDSM dungeons and you start seeing the same suspects.

There is probably an X-shaped cross somewhere. Maybe a spanking bench. Suspension points overhead. A cage that spends ninety percent of the evening being stared at. Somebody has built at least one piece of furniture that would make a Home Depot employee very concerned about what you are planning to do with the lumber.

There are rules too. Ask before touching. Negotiate before playing. Do not insert yourself into somebody else’s scene. Clean shared equipment when you are finished. Respect privacy. If something genuinely looks wrong, find the dungeon monitor, play monitor, DM, or whatever that particular event calls the person currently responsible for preventing a room full of horny adults from becoming a liability nightmare.

After enough time around these spaces, the setup starts to feel strangely standard. Of course there is a cross. Of course there is a bench. Of course watching somebody play does not mean you have been invited to join them.

So who decided a BDSM dungeon was supposed to look and operate like this?

As it turns out, nobody.

There was no Grand High Pervert who descended from a castle basement carrying the Official Dungeon Blueprint.

What we now recognize as a BDSM play space developed gradually through overlapping leather, S/M, fetish, professional domination and sexual-subculture communities. Gay leather culture played a particularly important role in the United States, but women, heterosexual and bisexual practitioners, professional dominants, lesbian S/M groups, pansexual organizations, craftspeople and later commercial clubs all contributed to what eventually became the modern dungeon.

It was considerably less organized at the beginning.

First, Everybody Had to Find Everybody Else

The postwar gay leather subculture began taking recognizable form in the United States during the 1950s.

Los Angeles’s Satyrs Motorcycle Club was founded in 1954 and today identifies itself as the oldest continuously operating gay men’s motorcycle club in the world. Clubs like the Satyrs provided durable social networks at a time when openly gathering as gay men carried considerable social and legal risk. The Satyrs also helped other motorcycle clubs develop by sharing organizational structures and bylaws.

Bars became another important part of the network.

San Francisco’s first leather bar was the Why Not, which opened in the Tenderloin in 1962. Later that year, the Tool Box opened at Fourth and Harrison and became the first leather bar in South of Market. SoMa would eventually develop an extraordinary concentration of leather bars, bathhouses, clubs and other businesses serving gay leathermen.

None of this means motorcycle clubs or leather bars were simply early versions of modern BDSM organizations. They were not. Leather identity, homosexuality, motorcycle culture and S/M overlapped in complicated ways, and not everybody involved in one was necessarily involved in all the others.

What they did provide were ways for people to find one another.

That matters when your sexual interests are sufficiently unpopular that “have you tried joining a Facebook group?” is still about half a century away.

Then the Perverts Started Forming Organizations

The early 1970s brought organizations explicitly centered on S/M.

In New York, Pat Bond placed personal ads in late 1970 looking for other masochists. The first person to answer was Fran Nowve, who was then using the pseudonym Terry Kolb. Together they formed the Eulenspiegel Society in 1971. It initially focused on masochists before members voted that August to include sadists as well.

Chicago Hellfire Club also formed in 1971 as a gay men’s S/M organization. Its early play parties took place in private homes, and its Inferno gatherings eventually grew into large multi-day events. Inferno V in 1976 was the first overnight edition.

The Society of Janus followed in San Francisco in August 1974, founded by Cynthia Slater and Larry Olsen as a BDSM education and support organization open across genders and sexual orientations. It still operates today.

Then Samois formed in San Francisco in 1978. It is generally recognized as the first public lesbian S/M organization in the United States. Members created social and educational programs, held public events and organized women’s play parties, first in private homes and later in spaces including the Catacombs.

Suddenly there were newsletters, classes, meetings, memberships, demonstrations, political arguments and organized play.

We invented bureaucracy for spanking.

Apparently nothing escapes committee formation.

No, Your Spanking Bench Is Probably Not Medieval

Modern dungeon furniture looks old as hell.

Stocks. Racks. Crosses. Cages. Restraint tables. Strange wooden contraptions with enough attachment points to make IKEA issue a restraining order.

That makes it very easy to imagine kinky people simply inherited all of this from ancient prisons and torture chambers.

The history is much more recent.

Design historian Tom Cubbin’s research into gay leather culture and domestic sex dungeons found that furnishing private dungeons with commercially produced, purpose-built BDSM equipment does not appear to have become widespread until the early 1970s. Whips, restraints and other fetish items existed much earlier, but large specialty furniture was harder to obtain.

Then people started manufacturing it.

A 1976 catalog called S&M Dungeon Devices offered elaborate BDSM furniture made by Bouchard Wood Products, which was associated with The Dungeon on Folsom Street in San Francisco. The catalog came in versions photographed with either male models or mixed-gender models.

Another operation, R.F.M., began producing bondage furniture near Los Angeles around 1975. Its maker described encountering improvised bondage setups involving beds, couches and ordinary household furniture and deciding better equipment was needed.

Which is wonderfully practical.

Apparently at some point someone looked at a person tied to a television stand and thought, For fuck’s sake, I can build something better than this.

Cubbin also found that some of R.F.M.’s visual inspiration came not from actual medieval torture devices but from bondage and torture imagery in mainstream adventure films. Those dramatic Hollywood racks then got reworked into equipment meant for actual recreational use.

That is an important distinction. A lot of dungeon furniture looks historical without having a clean historical lineage.

The BDSM St. Andrew’s cross is where the historical trail gets much less satisfying.

The X-shaped cross itself is obviously much older as Christian iconography, but I could not find reliable documentation identifying one inventor, one dungeon or one moment when it became standardized BDSM furniture.

Today it is everywhere.

How exactly it got there is fuzzier.

So if somebody gives you an elaborate origin story involving secret European leather masters carrying the sacred St. Andrew’s cross tradition through twelve generations, ask for a source before engraving it on a plaque.

Dedicated Play Spaces Changed Things

The Catacombs opened in San Francisco in 1975 as a semi-secret, referral-only gay men’s sex club particularly associated with fisting and S/M. Women were later admitted, and Patrick Califia began renting the space for women’s S/M parties in 1979.

The 15 Association formed in San Francisco in 1980 as a gay men’s BDSM and leather club. Its first play party, Scene One, took place that June in rented dungeon space. Around the beginning of 1981, the organization opened its first clubhouse. Social space occupied one level while the basement had been converted specifically for play, with crosses and bondage tables built into it.

That distinction is important.

People had obviously been creating private play environments long before 1981. But dedicated club spaces made it possible to design an environment around BDSM rather than continually adapting ordinary domestic rooms to accommodate it.

The dungeon increasingly became a destination instead of somebody’s creatively repurposed living room.

The Rules Were Built Too

There has never been one universal BDSM rulebook.

Different clubs developed different customs. They still do.

One venue may allow an activity another prohibits completely. Rules around sex, blood, sharps, fire, photography, substances, nudity, equipment and spectators can vary considerably. Even the staff watching the play space might be called dungeon monitors, DMs, play monitors or something else.

What became increasingly common was the idea that shared BDSM space requires explicit expectations around consent, privacy, sanitation, equipment and interference with other people’s scenes.

Modern Society of Janus rules are a good example of what this looks like now. Guests are told not to touch people or belongings without permission, to negotiate before play, not to interrupt scenes, to clean shared equipment and to contact a dungeon or play-space monitor when something is wrong.

Those current rules do not tell us exactly when every piece of dungeon etiquette originated. Different communities developed practices independently, borrowed from one another and changed them over time.

Even some of the language that now sounds ancient is fairly recent.

“Safe, sane and consensual” first appears in the historical record in an August 1983 statement produced by a Gay Male S/M Activists committee in New York. David Stein, who served on the committee, later wrote that this was the earliest use anyone had located and that he was probably the person who coined the phrasing.

Safe, sane and consensual is younger than Return of the Jedi.

I am not claiming this changes BDSM history in any meaningful way. I am saying I will never look at Yoda quite the same again.

The language kept evolving. In 1999, Gary Switch proposed RACK, Risk-Aware Consensual Kink, on a TES discussion list as an alternative to SSC, arguing that BDSM could not honestly be described as completely safe and that awareness of risk mattered more.

Kinksters have been arguing about terminology ever since.

Nature is healing.

AIDS Changed the Community and Its Rules

The AIDS epidemic devastated gay male communities, including the leather and S/M organizations that had built many of these institutions.

Chicago Hellfire Club lost members and responded by adopting safer-sex policies and monitoring play spaces. The 15 Association’s history describes the epidemic hitting its membership hard enough that its first clubhouse closed in 1982, although the organization continued holding play events elsewhere.

People within the leather and BDSM worlds were also actively involved in safer-sex education.

Cynthia Slater, one of the founders of the Society of Janus, became heavily involved in AIDS education. Kink educators developed workshops around reducing HIV transmission while preserving S/M and sexual expression rather than treating abstinence as the only available answer.

That helped push risk reduction, sexual-health education and explicit rules further into the foreground of organized leather and BDSM spaces.

It was not the beginning of safety culture, and it did not create modern consent practices by itself.

It was one enormous, horrifying pressure that forced communities to adapt.

Then the Perverts Got Modems

In 1991, the Usenet newsgroup alt.sex.bondage appeared online.

By 1994, Wired was describing it as a major online meeting place where people discussed technique, fantasies, relationships, safety and beginner questions.

For people with internet access, finding other kinky people no longer depended entirely on already knowing the right club, bar, organization or friend.

Then came the munch.

According to what researcher Margot Weiss specifically calls BDSM community legend, the first regular “burger munch” developed around Palo Alto in April 1992 after a woman known as STella invited people from alt.sex.bondage to meet socially. Kirk’s Steakburgers became the regular location, and the format spread.

That qualifier matters. There may well have been earlier informal kinky restaurant gatherings. What we can document is that this particular Northern California gathering became enormously influential in establishing the format and the term.

A munch was aggressively ordinary compared with a dungeon.

People ate food.

They talked.

Nobody needed to arrive in fetishwear or know how to use a single-tail whip without removing someone’s eyebrow.

Over time, munches became one of the ways people could enter local kink communities socially before deciding whether they wanted to attend play events.

The internet kept expanding that access through mailing lists, forums, websites and eventually dedicated social networks.

Finding your local freaks was becoming much easier.

So Who the Fuck Invented the Dungeon?

Still nobody.

And I think that answer is more interesting.

What we call a BDSM dungeon is the result of decades of people borrowing, building, organizing and changing things.

Gay leather communities were major contributors to the social networks and dedicated spaces that shaped modern American S/M. Pansexual organizations built education and support systems. Lesbian S/M groups created spaces of their own. Professional and recreational players experimented with equipment. Craftspeople started manufacturing furniture specifically for bondage and impact play. Clubs converted buildings into dedicated play spaces. Communities developed rules. AIDS changed risk practices. The internet made everybody easier to find.

It did not happen in a clean sequence, and nobody owns the entire history.

That is probably why trying to trace one piece of dungeon culture backward gets weird so quickly. Some traditions are well documented. Some evolved independently in several places. Some were passed around informally. Some have origin stories that sound extremely authoritative right up until you ask where anybody actually wrote them down.

And somehow, after all of that history, we ended up with a room containing an enormous wooden X, three carabiners nobody claims to own and at least one person standing nearby saying: “Actually, that is not what a St. Andrew’s cross was historically used for.”

There is always one.
